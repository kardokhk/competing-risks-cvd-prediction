# Figure captions, version 2

Target venue: JAMIA, Research and Applications.

Every figure in this directory is regenerated by `bash src/v2/figures/run_all.sh`
from the CSVs under `results/metrics_v2/`, `results/closed_form/` and
`results/deep/`. Nothing is recomputed and nothing is edited by hand. Each figure
is written as a PDF master, a 600 dpi PNG, an 800 dpi flattened LZW TIFF, the
plotted values as `<stem>_data.csv`, and two inspection images
(`_actualsize.png`, `_grey.png`) that are not deliverables. JAMIA wants figures
as separate files cited in numerical order, which these are.

**Alt text is mandatory at JAMIA and is provided twice**: as an `**Alt text.**`
paragraph under each caption below, and machine-readably in
`figures/v2/alt_text.csv`, columns `figure`, `file`, `alt_text`. Both are written
from one source, `src/v2/figures/alt_text.py`, so they cannot drift apart. Re-run
that script after any change to a figure's appearance: alt text describes the
image and will otherwise go stale without warning.

**Figure budget.** JAMIA allows 6 figures and 4 tables in a Research and
Applications paper. Figures 1 to 4 are the main text, inside the budget; Figures
S1 to S8 go to the online supplement, which is not capped. Nothing else should be
promoted to the main text without checking the budget again.

## Conventions used in every figure

- **Naive** means the competing death was treated as censoring, so the model
  targets 1 - S(t). **Competing risk** means the competing death was modelled, so
  the model targets the cause-1 cumulative incidence. Within a learner-matched
  pair nothing else differs: the same learner, the same 11 predictors, the same
  standardisation, no tuning of either member.
- The naive-versus-competing distinction is carried by colour, by marker shape
  and by line style together, so no comparison depends on colour alone.
- Every interval is a 95% percentile interval from a paired shared-index
  bootstrap with B = 1,000 replicates, pooled across m = 30 multiple imputations
  (Schomaker and Heumann MI Boot PS), unless a caption says otherwise. The point
  estimate is Qbar, the mean over imputations of the estimate on the unbootstrapped
  imputed data, never the mean of the bootstrap distribution. Both the percentile
  and the Rubin interval are in `results/metrics_v2/main_metrics.csv`; the
  percentile interval is plotted because E/O is a ratio and a net benefit near
  zero is bounded below.
- **Temporal split**: models trained on NHANES 1999-2006, evaluated on 2007-2010,
  n = 9,329, observed Aalen-Johansen 10-year cumulative incidence of
  cardiovascular death 3.30% (2.93 to 3.67), fraction of the sample still
  informative at 10 years 77.9%.
- **Cross-validated split**: three repeats of out-of-fold prediction over the
  whole 1999-2018 cohort, n = 41,151, observed 10-year cumulative incidence 3.68%
  (3.48 to 3.89), at-risk fraction 55.3%.
- Every symbol key is drawn inside the artwork rather than described here, and
  threshold tiers are marked with a rule rather than a shaded tint. Both were
  Statistics in Medicine requirements and are kept under JAMIA: they cost
  nothing, and a key in the panel survives a figure being read apart from its
  caption.

## Figure 1. Competing-risk models track the observed incidence; their naive partners sit below it

**Caption.** Naive models over-predict the 10-year cumulative incidence of
cardiovascular death, and their competing-risk partners do not, for all three
learner-matched pairs. Flexible calibration on the temporal test set (models
trained on NHANES 1999-2006, evaluated on 2007-2010; n = 9,329; 303 cardiovascular
deaths and 77.9% of the sample still informative at 10 years). The x axis is the
predicted 10-year cumulative incidence and the y axis the observed one, so a
model that over-predicts falls **below** the dotted identity line. (**A**) Cox:
`cox_naive`, which censors the competing death, against `csc_cox`, the
cause-specific Cox model, which models it. (**B**) Random survival forest,
`rsf_naive` against `rsf_cr`. (**C**) Inverse-probability-of-censoring-weighted
binomial regression, `logistic` against `logistic_cr`. Each pair shares its
learner, its predictors and its hyperparameters; only the treatment of the
competing death differs. Curves are the flexible calibration curve on 100
percentile bins of predicted risk, truncated for display at the 98th percentile,
so the top 2% of each model's predictions is off the panel; the full curve is in
`results/metrics_v2/calibration/smooth__temporal__h10.csv`. Points are the ten
deciles of predicted risk (n = 933 per decile), plotted at the decile mean
prediction against the Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence within the decile,
with 95% intervals. **Open markers with dotted intervals are deciles carrying
fewer than 15 cardiovascular deaths** (`flag_sparse` in the source file; deciles 1
to 6 here); they are shown rather than dropped, and they should not be read as
evidence. The strip below each panel is a quantile rug: one tick per percentile
of predicted risk, naive above and competing risk below. Annotations give the
expected-over-observed ratio and the integrated calibration index with their 95%
percentile intervals, both computed on the pseudo-observation smoother of van
Geloven et al. (BMJ 2022;377:e069249). Source data:
`figures/v2/fig01_calibration_data.csv`. Generated by
`src/v2/figures/fig01_calibration.py`.

**What the reader should conclude.** Ignoring the competing risk moves the
predicted level substantially and in a consistent direction, and it does so for
every learner, not only for the Cox model.

**Alt text.** Three square panels side by side, each plotting predicted against observed 10-year cumulative incidence from 0 to 30% with a dotted diagonal for perfect calibration. In every panel a dashed orange curve with circles runs below the diagonal and a solid blue curve with squares runs closer to it, so the naive model over-predicts in all three. Open markers flag deciles with too few events. A rug strip under each panel puts most predicted risk below 10%.

## Figure 2. The same comparison is decisive on calibration and null on decisions

**Caption.** Ignoring competing risks changes predicted risk measurably and does
not change net benefit detectably at any threshold appropriate to this endpoint.
All panels: temporal test set, 10-year horizon, n = 9,329; every contrast is the
naive member minus the competing-risk member of a learner-matched pair, paired
within bootstrap replicate and imputation; error bars are 95% percentile
intervals. **Marker fill is the same encoding throughout: filled means the
interval excludes zero, open means it contains zero.**
(**A**) Difference in expected over observed. (**B**) Difference in the
calibration slope, estimated by the pseudo-observation model of van Geloven et
al. with a complementary log-log mean link. (**C**) Difference in the integrated
calibration index, expressed per 1,000. All nine calibration contrasts exclude
zero. (**D**) Difference in net benefit at the six pre-specified decision
thresholds, expressed as net true positives per 1,000 people screened. The
thresholds are tiered because 7.5% and 20% are defined on incident
atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease whereas the endpoint here is
cardiovascular mortality, whose observed 10-year cumulative incidence in this
cohort is 33.0 per 1,000: 1, 2, 3 and 5% are primary and 7.5% is secondary.
**Every one of these fifteen intervals contains zero.** The supplement-only 20%
threshold is not on this panel and is restored, at the same scale as everything
else, in Figure S2; it is the only threshold at which any learner-matched
contrast excludes zero, and putting it on a common axis with the rest compresses
the thresholds that carry the argument into a band of dots. (**E**) The fraction of the sample
above each threshold: the primary Cox pair as markers, and the range across all
ten models as a vertical rule. The two contrasts that are not learner-matched
(`cox_naive - fine_gray` and `gbs_naive - deephit`) are excluded from this figure
and appear in Figure S1; a difference between models that do not share a learner
is not evidence about competing risks. Decision curves carry no simultaneous
band: the intervals that carry the argument are at the pre-specified thresholds
shown here. Source data: `figures/v2/fig02_two_halves_data.csv`. Generated by
`src/v2/figures/fig02_two_halves.py`.

**What the reader should conclude.** The same paired comparison that is decisive
on every level of the calibration hierarchy is null on net benefit everywhere the
endpoint's own risk distribution puts patients. Calibration and decision impact
are not the same question, and a paper that reports only the first overstates the
consequences of the second.

**Alt text.** Five panels. Three small forest plots down the left show differences in E/O, calibration slope and calibration index for three model pairs; all nine intervals sit clear of the vertical zero line, positive for the first and third, negative for slope. The large panel at right plots the difference in net benefit at five thresholds: every interval crosses the horizontal zero line and every marker is open. Below it, the fraction above threshold falls from about 60% to 20%.

## Figure 3. A closed form accounts for the whole of the naive-versus-competing gap

**Caption.** The difference in mean predicted risk between a naive and a
cause-specific Cox model is fully accounted for by a zero-parameter identity, and
its dominant term is heterogeneity, not the marginal competing-event rate.
Complete-case temporal test set, n = 8,096 (this stage predates the multiple
imputation used in Figures 1 and 2, so its n differs from theirs). With
p = F1 + F2 and pi = F1 / p, under proportional cause-specific hazards the naive
risk is A = 1 - (1 - p)^pi and the gap is G = A - F1; the derivation and its
assumptions are in `results/closed_form/derivation.md`.
(**A**) Each term of the decomposition of the observed difference at 10 years,
as a percentage of that difference: change of estimand evaluated at the cohort's
mean incidences 32.0%, heterogeneity (Jensen) term 72.2%, hazard timing -3.7%,
implementation (scikit-survival Breslow baseline against R `coxph` with Efron
ties) -0.5%. Bars are the 10-year values; whiskers are 95% intervals from a
300-replicate bootstrap that resamples the training set, refits the
cause-specific Cox model and resamples the test set
(`results/closed_form/decomposition_refit_boot.csv`), and are intervals on the
share of the model-only difference D_model, which differs from the observed
difference by the implementation term, at most 1.1%. The implementation term is
deterministic and carries no interval. Open triangles are the same shares at 5
and 15 years. (**B**) The nomogram: contours of the pooled naive inflation
A / F1 over a 7,200-point grid, F1 from 0.5 to 30% and F2 from 0.5 to 60% in 0.5%
steps with F1 + F2 < 1. Open circles are the 65 NHANES strata with at least 15
cause-1 events, from 90 strata built on the 35,309 complete cases of NHANES
1999-2018 (5-year age band by horizon; sex by 10-year age band by horizon; decile
of out-of-fold predicted risk by horizon); the 25 insufficient strata are not
plotted and are flagged `sufficient = FALSE` in
`results/closed_form/validation.csv`. Filled diamonds are the pooled temporal
test set at 5, 10 and 15 years. The star is an external check on a published
cohort that has nothing to do with this one: Truchot et al. (J Am Soc Nephrol
2025;36:688-701, doi:10.1681/asn.0000000517), 11,046 kidney transplant
recipients, median follow-up 4.7 years, 1,497 graft losses (F1 = 14%) against
1,003 deaths with a functioning graft (F2 = 9%), event counts and
Kaplan-Meier/Aalen-Johansen values taken from the published abstract via the
Europe PMC record, accessed 2026-09-04. At those two incidences the identity
gives an inflation of 1.051, against the published Kaplan-Meier to
Aalen-Johansen ratio of 1.0625, an absolute error of 0.012. **That check is at
the same pooled, marginal level as the grid**: it tests whether the identity
reproduces a Kaplan-Meier-versus-Aalen-Johansen discrepancy computed on marginal
rates, and it says nothing about a model-to-model difference, which additionally
requires the heterogeneity term. Values from
`results/closed_form/external_check_truchot.csv`. Within a stratum every quantity is marginal, so
heterogeneity does not enter and the only assumption under test is
proportionality of the two cause-specific hazards; the median relative error of
the identity over the 65 strata is -1.24% (interquartile range -6.00 to +6.23%,
5th to 95th percentile -14.37 to +28.93%). (**C**) How much of the observed
difference each way of using the closed form recovers. Evaluated at the cohort's
mean incidences it recovers 34.5, 32.0 and 30.7% at 5, 10 and 15 years; evaluated
per subject and averaged it recovers 112.4, 104.2 and 99.1%. The ratio between
the two, annotated, is 3.26, 3.25 and 3.23. The interval is the refit bootstrap
interval on the pooled share; the per-subject share is a sum of two shares whose
joint bootstrap distribution is not recorded, so it is plotted without one.

**The nomogram in B is pooled.** It gives the inflation for a group whose members
all carry the same F1 and F2. For a cohort with heterogeneous risk the mean
per-subject gap is larger, here by a factor of 3.25 at 10 years (panel C),
because an individual's F1 and F2 are strongly correlated (Pearson r = 0.930,
95% CI 0.925 to 0.934 at 10 years): the grid omits exactly the heterogeneity term
that supplies 72% of the gap. **A reader who takes a cohort's marginal F1 and F2
to this grid will understate the naive-versus-competing difference roughly
threefold. Read panel B as a lower bound, not as a correction factor.** The same
caution applies to the external check plotted on it, which sits at that same
pooled, marginal level.

Source data: `figures/v2/fig03_closed_form_data.csv`.
Generated by `src/v2/figures/fig03_closed_form.py`.

**What the reader should conclude.** The bias from ignoring competing risks is
not an empirical regularity to be tabulated; it is an algebraic consequence of
changing the estimand, and the quantity that governs its size is the
within-individual correlation between event risk and competing-event risk
(Pearson r = 0.930, 95% CI 0.925 to 0.934 at 10 years), not the marginal
competing-event rate.

**Alt text.** Three panels. Top left, five bars: a hatched reference bar at 100%, then heterogeneity tallest of the four components near 72%, change of estimand about a third, and two near zero. Right, a tall scatter of competing-event against event incidence in percent, crossed by five gently sloping contours labelled 1.05 to 1.60; open circles rise diagonally, with three filled diamonds low down and one star at 14 by 9%. Bottom left, at three horizons a square near 30% and a triangle near 100%, joined by a line marked about 3.25 times.

## Figure 4. DeepHit's calibration is set by one hyperparameter, and the cost of its default is visible in the fitted models

**Caption.** Moving the weight the DeepHit loss puts on its likelihood term
changes calibration by more than an order of magnitude and leaves discrimination
flat, and the two deep competing-risk models differ in calibration but not in
discrimination when both are fitted as they are everywhere else in this paper.
Columns are paired by metric: A with D, B with E, C with F.

**The two rows come from different fits, and the distinction matters.** A-C are a
**separate experiment**, run before seed ensembling: the architecture, optimiser
and early-stopping rule were held fixed and **15 single fits** were made at each
alpha setting. Sigma, which enters only the ranking term, is held at 0.1, the
value with the complete alpha grid. D-F are the **production fits, 10-member seed
ensembles**, the same objects that appear in Figure 2, Figure S1 and the tables.
The two rows are therefore not comparable with each other: the ICI values in A
are not comparable with those in D, nor with any ICI reported elsewhere in the
paper, and **only D-F are comparable with Figure 2 and the tables**. A reader
scanning two rows of one figure will otherwise assume one provenance.

(**A**) Integrated calibration index against alpha, log scale; the median falls
from 0.158 at alpha = 0, pure ranking, to 0.0036 at alpha = 1, pure likelihood.
(**B**) Expected over observed, log scale, dashed rule at 1; the median falls
from 4.80 to 1.07. (**C**) Truncated IPCW concordance, linear scale: the median
is 0.831 to 0.834 at every alpha from 0.05 upward, and the pure ranking loss is
the worst discriminator at 0.826. In A-C, points are medians over replicate fits
(30 replicates at alpha = 0 and alpha = 1, 15 at the intermediate settings on the
inner out-of-fold protocol; 10 and 5 respectively on the temporal test set) and
whiskers span the 10th to 90th percentile of those replicates, **not** a
confidence interval. The dotted vertical rule marks alpha = 0.2, the value used
in the prior work this benchmark follows. An internal check that the sweep
isolated what it was meant to: at alpha = 1 the four values of sigma give
identical results to four decimal places, as they must when the ranking term
carries no weight.

(**D**-**F**) The paired difference between the two deep models at 10 years,
DeepHit minus Neural Fine-Gray, on both splits, with 95% percentile intervals
from the same shared-index bootstrap as Figure 2; **a filled marker means the
95% interval excludes zero and an open marker means it contains zero, as in
Figure 2**. Calibration
differs and discrimination does not. **E/O excludes zero on both splits**, 0.094
(0.024 to 0.177) on the temporal split and 0.091 (0.077 to 0.113) on the
cross-validated one. **ICI excludes zero on the temporal split only**, 3.10
(0.56 to 5.56) per 1,000 against 1.90 (-1.66 to 3.59) per 1,000 under
cross-validation. Neither the calibration slope nor the concordance excludes zero
on either split. Both models are 10-member seed ensembles of competing-risk deep
learners, so **this contrast isolates the training objective**, a
ranking-weighted likelihood against a monotone cumulative-incidence
parameterisation, and not the competing-risk assumption; it is not
learner-matched in the sense used for the naive-versus-competing pairs in
Figure 2, and it is labelled `secondary` and `learner_matched = False` in
`paired_contrasts.csv`. Source data: `figures/v2/fig04_alpha_sweep_data.csv`.
Generated by `src/v2/figures/fig04_alpha_sweep.py`.

**What the reader should conclude.** A benchmark that reports a deep
competing-risk model as badly calibrated is reporting its default loss weighting,
not its architecture; and once both deep models are fitted properly, what
separates them is calibration alone.

**Alt text.** Six panels in two rows of three, paired by column. The top row plots three measures against a loss weight from 0 to 1: on log axes the calibration index and the expected-over-observed ratio fall steeply then flatten, while concordance stays flat near 0.83 throughout. The bottom row shows the same measures as paired differences for two validation designs; the calibration markers sit right of the vertical zero line, one interval still crossing it, and the concordance markers straddle zero.

## Figure S1. Discrimination is the same for every model

**Caption.** No model discriminates better than any other, and no
naive-versus-competing contrast on discrimination excludes zero. Ten-year
horizon. (**A**) Inverse-probability-of-censoring-weighted time-dependent AUC for
all ten models. Marker shape carries the family, as in Figures 1 to 3 (circle
naive, square competing risk), marker fill carries the split (filled temporal,
open cross-validated), and the dashed rule separates the two families, so
neither distinction depends on colour. Censoring weights are estimated within

triangles concordance, and marker fill follows Figure 2 (filled excludes zero). The two starred contrasts are **not** learner-matched and are drawn in

`gbs_naive - deephit` compares a gradient-boosted Cox model with a deep ranking
model and shares no learner at all. They are shown because the pairing structure is what

`figures/v2/figS1_discrimination_data.csv`. Generated by
`src/v2/figures/figS1_discrimination.py`.

**Alt text.** Three panels. The first two are forest plots of time-dependent AUC and of concordance for ten models, every point clustered between 0.80 and 0.85 with heavily overlapping intervals, and a dashed horizontal rule separating the four naive models above from the six competing-risk models below. The third plots paired differences for five contrasts: every interval crosses the vertical zero line, and the two contrasts that are not learner-matched are drawn in grey at the bottom with much wider intervals.

## Figure S3. The shared over-prediction on the temporal split is secular drift

20 years of follow-up. (**B**) The naive-to-competing E/O ratio for the same

(1.149, computed as (mean F1 + mean per-subject gap) / mean F1 from

1.143 temporally at 10 years. Source data: `figures/v2/figS3_drift_data.csv`.
Generated by `src/v2/figures/figS3_drift.py`.

**Alt text.** Two panels. Left, expected over observed for two Cox models under two validation designs at three horizons: the out-of-fold points sit on the dashed reference line at 1 while the temporal points sit well above it, and two open markers with dotted intervals at 15 years are flagged as not estimable. Right, the ratio between the two models rises from about 1.05 at 5 years to about 1.28 at 15, with a horizontal reference line near 1.15.

## Figure S8. The random seed, not the imputation, was driving the deep models' between-imputation spread

**Caption.** Fitting one deep model per imputed dataset records the random seed as
if it were missing-data uncertainty, and averaging over a 10-member seed ensemble
removes most of it. (**A**) For each of seven statistics at 10 years on the
temporal split, the share of the spread across a single-fit-per-imputation run
that is attributable to the seed rather than to the imputation, computed as
sd_seed / sd_single_seed_run from `results/deep/instability_decomposition.csv`
(30 imputations, 10 seeds). It lies between 93% and 102% for every statistic in
both models. A value above 100% is possible because these are ratios of two
separately estimated standard deviations, not a partition of one. The pure
imputation component of the mean predicted risk at 10 years is 0.00 per 1,000 for
DeepHit and 0.64 per 1,000 for Neural Fine-Gray; that is, for DeepHit the
imputation contributed nothing measurable to the spread of its own mean
prediction. (**B**) The fraction of missing information for all 380 pooled
estimands of the two models, across both splits and all three horizons, before
and after ensembling (`results/deep/fmi_before_after.csv`). Marker shape is the
model, as in A; fill is the split. Points below the dotted identity line are
estimands whose FMI fell: 281 fell, 90 were unchanged and 9 rose, the median went
from 0.175 to 0.039, and the number requiring more than 30 imputations by von
Hippel's rule fell from 125 to 83. **Four estimands remain above 0.9 after
ensembling** (circled): every one is the fraction of the sample above the 1%
threshold, and they sit at 0.92 to 0.94, so they still call for far more
imputations than the 30 that were run and should be quoted with that caveat.
Source data: `figures/v2/figS8_instability_data.csv`. Generated by
`src/v2/figures/figS8_instability.py`.

**What the reader should conclude.** A benchmark that fits one stochastic learner
per imputed dataset and pools with Rubin's rules will attribute the learner's own
seed variance to missing data, inflating both the fraction of missing information
and the interval. For these two models the misattribution was almost total.

**Alt text.** Two panels. Left, seven statistics along the x axis, each carrying two markers that all fall between 93 and 102% against a dashed rule at 100%, so the seed accounts for nearly the whole of the spread. Right, a square scatter of the fraction of missing information before ensembling against after, 0 to 1.1 on both axes: almost every point lies well below the dotted diagonal, and four circled points remain near the top right corner.
